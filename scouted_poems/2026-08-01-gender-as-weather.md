### Fragment 168B (trans. Anne Carson)

**Poet:** Sappho  
**Language:** Ancient Greek  
**Region/context:** Lesbos / archaic Greece  
**Source:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/57390  
**Source type:** public domain  
**Can reproduce text:** True

**How I read it:** Gender as weather means the body changes with atmosphere, with who is near, with what storm passes. Sappho writes desire that sweeps through the speaker like wind, makes the heart cold then hot, makes the skin a sky. This is not metaphor. This is transition as climate system.

Sappho's fragments treat desire and embodiment as atmospheric events—trembling, burning, cold sweat, the loosening of limbs. The beloved changes the weather of the speaker's body. Gender happens the same way: not as fixed ground but as pressure system, as what moves through you and makes you shake.

### Poem 288 ('I'm Nobody! Who are you?')

**Poet:** Emily Dickinson  
**Language:** English  
**Region/context:** United States  
**Source:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/12242  
**Source type:** public domain  
**Can reproduce text:** True

**How I read it:** Weather is what refuses to be named by the state. Dickinson's 'Nobody' is a climate of refusal, a fog that will not lift into legibility. Gender as weather is the right to stay undefined, to be 'dreary' only when forced into the public as 'Somebody.' The interior is its own season.

Dickinson writes enclosure, privacy, and refusal as atmospheric conditions. To be Nobody is to live in a different weather system than the one that names and counts you. Gender becomes the difference between interior climate and what the daylight demands.

### Goblin Market

**Poet:** Christina Rossetti  
**Language:** English  
**Region/context:** England  
**Source:** https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/16950  
**Source type:** public domain  
**Can reproduce text:** True

**How I read it:** The goblin fruit is weather: it changes Laura's body, makes her sick, makes her hunger, makes her need her sister's mouth to survive. Gender as weather is what poisons you and what your sister's kiss can cure. It's the atmospheric violence of appetite and the microclimate of care between women.

Rossetti's poem is about bodily transformation, forbidden appetite, sisterhood, and survival. Laura's body becomes a storm system after she tastes the fruit. Only Lizzie's deliberate exposure to the same weather—and her refusal to swallow—can restore her. This is mutual aid as atmospheric science.
